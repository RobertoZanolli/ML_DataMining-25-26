## Association rules → discovering co–occurrencies in a market basket

Given a set of commercial transactions, find rules that will predict the occurrence of an item based on the occurrences of other items in the transaction

### Example of association rules

- `{Diaper} → {Beer}`
- `{Bread, Milk} → {Coke, Eggs}`
- `{Beer, Bread} → {Milk}`

> **Implication means co-occurrence, not causality!**

The implication of Association Rules is different from that of logic (boolean): **it can be true with some level of truth**

### Market basket transactions

### Definition: frequent Itemset

> **Itemset**  
> A collection of one or more items  
> *Example*: `{Bread, Diaper, Milk}`

> **k-itemset**  
> An itemset that contains $k$ items

> **Support count ($\sigma$)**  
> Frequency of occurrence of an itemset  
> *E.g.* $\sigma(\text{{\{Bread, Diaper, Milk\}}}) = 2$

> **Support**  
> Fraction of transactions that contain an itemset  
> *E.g.* support of `{Bread, Diaper, Milk}` $= \frac{2}{5}$

> **Frequent Itemset**  
> An itemset whose support is greater than or equal to a minsup threshold

### Definition: association rule

> **Association Rule**  
> An expression of the form $A \rightarrow C$, where $A$ and $C$ are itemsets  
> $A$ = Antecedent and $C$ = Consequent  
> *Example*: `{Diaper, Milk} → {Beer}`

#### Rule Evaluation Metrics

> **Support ($\text{sup}$)**  
> Fraction of the $N$ transactions that contain both $A$ and $C$

> **Confidence ($\text{conf}$)**  
> Measures how often all the items in $C$ appear in transactions that contain $A$

$$ \text{support}(A \rightarrow C) = \frac{\sigma(A \cup C)}{N} $$

$$ \text{confidence}(A \rightarrow C) = \frac{\sigma(A \cup C)}{\sigma(A)} $$

**Example:**
- For rule `{Diaper, Milk} → {Beer}`:
  - $\sigma(\text{{\{Beer, Diaper, Milk\}}}) = 2$
  - $\sigma(\text{{\{Diaper, Milk\}}}) = 2$
  - $N = 5$
  - $\text{support} = \frac{2}{5} = 0.4$
  - $\text{confidence} = \frac{2}{2} = 1.0$

| 3 | Beer, Coke, Diaper, Milk |
| 4 | Beer, Bread, Diaper, Milk |
| 5 | Bread, Coke, Diaper, Milk |

> **Why support and confidence?**
> - Rules with low support can be generated by random associations
> - Rules with low confidence are not really reliable
> - Nevertheless a rule with relatively low support but high confidence can represent an uncommon but interesting phenomenon

## Association rule mining task

Given a set of transactions $N$, the goal of association rule mining is to find all rules having:
- **support** ≥ minsup threshold  
- **confidence** ≥ minconf threshold

### Brute-force approach:
1. List all possible association rules
2. Compute the support and confidence for each rule
3. Prune rules that fail the minsup and minconf thresholds

> **This is computationally prohibitive!**

## Mining association rules

### Example of rules from itemset `{Beer, Diaper, Milk}`:

| Rule | Support ($s$) | Confidence ($c$) |
|------|---------------|------------------|
| `{Diaper, Milk} → {Beer}` | 0.4 | 0.67 |
| `{Beer, Milk} → {Diaper}` | 0.4 | 1.0 |
| `{Beer, Diaper} → {Milk}` | 0.4 | 0.67 |
| `{Beer} → {Diaper, Milk}` | 0.4 | 0.67 |
| `{Diaper} → {Beer, Milk}` | 0.4 | 0.5 |
| `{Milk} → {Beer, Diaper}` | 0.4 | 0.5 |

> **Key observation:**  
> All the rules above are binary partitions of the same itemset: `{Beer, Diaper, Milk}`

> **Important property:**  
> Rules originating from the same itemset have **identical support** but can have **different confidence**

> **Implication:**  
> We may decouple the support and confidence requirements

## Two-step approach for efficient mining

### 1. Frequent itemset generation
Generate all itemsets whose support is greater than minsup

### 2. Rule generation
Generate high confidence rules from each frequent itemset, where each rule is a binary partitioning of a frequent itemset

> **Note:**  
> Even with this decoupling, **frequent itemset generation is still computationally expensive**

## Frequent itemset generation

> Given $D$ items, there are $M = 2^D$ possible candidate itemsets
![](theory/images/Screenshot%202025-12-26%20alle%2016.55.28.png)

### Brute-force approach

- Each itemset in the lattice is a candidate frequent itemset
- Count the support of each candidate by scanning the database
- Match each transaction against every candidate

$$
\text{Complexity: } O(N \cdot W \cdot M) \implies \text{Expensive!}
$$

| TID | Items |
|-----|-------|
| 1 | Bread, Milk |
| 2 | Beer, Bread, Diaper, Eggs |
| 3 | Beer, Coke, Diaper, Milk |
| 4 | Beer, Bread, Diaper, Milk |
| 5 | Bread, Coke, Diaper, Milk |

*(Where $N$ = number of transactions, $W$ = average transaction width, $M$ = number of candidate itemsets)*

### Brute-force computational complexity (OPTIONAL!!!)

Given $D$ unique items:

$$
\text{Total number of itemsets} = 2^D
$$

$$
\text{Total number of possible association rules:}
$$

$$
R = \sum_{k=1}^{D-1} \left[ \binom{D}{k} \times \sum_{j=1}^{D-k} \binom{D-k}{j} \right] = 3^D - 2^{D+1} + 1
$$


1. Count the number of ways to create an itemset that forms the left-hand side of the rule
2. For each size $k$ itemset selected for the left-hand side, count the number of ways to choose the remaining $D-k$ items to form the right-hand side of the rule

**Going deeper:**

- Choose $k$ of the $D$ items for the left-hand side of the rule: there are $\binom{D}{k}$ ways to do this
- There are $\binom{D-k}{i}$ ways to choose the right-hand side of the rule, where $1 \leq i \leq D-k$
- The double summation derives from the two points above
- The binomial theorem states that $\sum_{i=0}^{n} \binom{n}{i} x^i = (1 + x)^n$
- Using the theorem for $x=1$ and $x=2$ leads to the final result (pay attention to the starting value of the summation)
## Frequent itemset generation strategies

### Goal: Reduce computational complexity

#### 1. Reduce the number of candidates $M$
- Complete search: $M = 2^D$
- **Use pruning techniques to reduce $M$**

#### 2. Reduce the number of comparisons $N \cdot M$
- Use efficient data structures to store the candidates or transactions
- No need to match every candidate against every transaction

## The Apriori principle

> **Apriori principle:**  
> If an itemset is frequent, then all of its subsets must also be frequent

**Why does this hold?**  
Due to the anti-monotone property of the support measure:

$$
\forall X, Y : (X \subseteq Y) \implies \text{support}(X) \geq \text{support}(Y)
$$

> **Key property:**  
> The support of an itemset never exceeds the support of its subsets
> 
> This is known as the **anti-monotone property** of support

**Consequences for pruning:**
- If any subset of an itemset is **infrequent**, the itemset itself must also be **infrequent**
- We can prune candidate itemsets early by checking their subsets
- This dramatically reduces the search space in frequent itemset mining
  ![](theory/images/Screenshot%202025-12-26%20alle%2017.07.39.png)
- ## Apriori algorithm - candidate generation

### Definitions

- $C_k$: candidate itemsets of size $k$
- $L_k$: frequent itemsets of size $k$
- $\text{subset}_k(c)$: set of the subsets of $c$ with $k$ elements

## Candidate generation – join step

Let $L_k$ be represented as a table with $k$ columns where each row is a frequent itemset.

Let the items in each row of $L_k$ be in **lexicographic order**.

$C_{k+1}$ is generated by a **self join** of $L_k$:

```sql
INSERT INTO C_{k+1}
SELECT p.item1, p.item2, ..., p.item_k, q.item_k
FROM L_k AS p, L_k AS q
WHERE p.item1 = q.item1 
  AND p.item2 = q.item2 
  ...
  AND p.item_{k-1} = q.item_{k-1}
  AND p.item_k < q.item_k;
```

> **How it works:**
> - Take two frequent $k$-itemsets from $L_k$
> - They must share the first $k-1$ items (ensures proper merging)
> - The last items must be different, with $p.item_k < q.item_k$ (avoid duplicates)
> - This creates a candidate $(k+1)$-itemset by combining them

## Origin of the name "Apriori"

> **Level-wise computation:**
> - The level is the cardinality of the itemsets under evaluation
> - The evaluations at level $k$ use the **prior knowledge** acquired for the previous levels to reduce the search space

## Factors affecting complexity

### 1. Choice of minimum support threshold
- Lowering support threshold → more frequent itemsets
- This reduces pruning effectiveness and increases maximum length of frequent itemsets
- **Number of database scans = maximum length of frequent itemsets + 1**

### 2. Dimensionality (number of items) of the dataset
- More items → more space needed to store support counts
- If number of frequent items increases → both computation and I/O costs increase

### 3. Size of database
- Since Apriori makes multiple passes, runtime increases with number of transactions

### 4. Average transaction width
- Denser datasets → wider transactions
- Wider transactions → increase maximum length of frequent itemsets
- **Why?** Number of subsets in a transaction grows exponentially with its width

## Confidence

> The confidence of a rule can be computed from the supports:
> 
> $$
> \text{conf}(A \rightarrow C) = \frac{\text{support}(A \cup C)}{\text{support}(A)}
> $$

> **Key insight:**  
> For confidence-based pruning of rules, it is sufficient to know the supports of frequent itemsets

## Rule generation

### Step 1: Generate candidate rules
Given a frequent itemset $L$, find all non-empty subsets $f \subset L$ such that:

$$
\text{conf}(f \rightarrow (L - f)) \geq \text{minconf}
$$

**Example:** From `{Beer, Diaper, Milk}`, the possible rules are:
- `Beer, Diaper → Milk`
- `Beer → Diaper, Milk`
- `Beer, Milk → Diaper`
- `Milk → Beer, Diaper`
- `Diaper, Milk → Beer`
- `Diaper → Beer, Milk`

> If $|L| = k$, there are $2^k - 2$ candidate rules  
> (Rules $L \rightarrow \emptyset$ and $\emptyset \rightarrow L$ can be ignored)

## Efficient rule generation

### Challenge
In general, confidence does **not** have an anti-monotone property:

$$
\text{conf}(ABC \rightarrow D) \text{ can be larger or smaller than } \text{conf}(AB \rightarrow D)
$$

### Key observation for same itemset
Consider rules generated from the same itemset, e.g., $I = \{A, B, C, D\} \in L$:

$$
\text{conf}(ABC \rightarrow D) \geq \text{conf}(AB \rightarrow CD) \geq \text{conf}(A \rightarrow BCD)
$$

> **Important property:**  
> Confidence of rules generated from the same itemset is **anti-monotone** with respect to the number of items on the RHS of the rule
> 
> It **decreases** when we move an item from the left-hand side to the right-hand side


## Rule generation in Apriori

### Candidate rule generation
A candidate rule is generated by **merging two rules** that share the same prefix in the rule consequent.

**Example:**
- Rule 1: `CD → AB`
- Rule 2: `BD → AC`

Join these to produce candidate rule: `D → ABC`

### Pruning rule candidates
Prune rule `D → ABC` if its subset `AD → BC` does not have high confidence.

> **Why?**  
> Based on the anti-monotone property of confidence within the same itemset:  
> If a more specific rule (with more items on LHS) has low confidence,  
> a more general rule (with fewer items on LHS) cannot have higher confidence.

## Pattern evaluation

### The problem with association rules
Association rule algorithms tend to produce **too many rules**:
- Many are uninteresting or redundant

**Example of redundancy:**
- `{A, B, C} → {D}` and `{A, B} → {D}` have same support and confidence
- The first rule doesn't add meaningful information beyond the second

### Interestingness measures
- Can be used to **prune/rank** derived patterns
- Help identify truly interesting associations

> **Note:**  
> In the original formulation of association rules, **support** and **confidence** are the only measures used


## Computing interestingness measures

Given a rule $A \rightarrow C$, the information needed to compute rule interestingness can be obtained from a **contingency table**:

| | $C$ | $\neg C$ | Total |
|------|-----|--------|-------|
| $A$ | $f_{11}$ | $f_{10}$ | $f_{1\cdot}$ |
| $\neg A$ | $f_{01}$ | $f_{00}$ | $f_{0\cdot}$ |
| Total | $f_{\cdot 1}$ | $f_{\cdot 0}$ | |

> The elements of the contingency table are the basis for most interestingness measures

## Drawback of confidence

**Example contingency table:**

| | Coffee | $\neg$ Coffee | Total |
|------|--------|--------------|-------|
| Tea | 15 | 5 | 20 |
| $\neg$ Tea | 75 | 5 | 80 |
| Total | 90 | 10 | 100 |

$$
\text{conf}(\text{Tea} \rightarrow \text{Coffee}) = \frac{\text{supp}(\text{Tea, Coffee})}{\text{supp}(\text{Tea})} = \frac{15}{20} = 0.75
$$

- $P(\text{Coffee}) = 0.9$
- $P(\text{Coffee} \mid \neg \text{Tea}) = \frac{75}{80} = 0.9375$

> **Problem:** Despite high confidence of `Tea → Coffee`, the absence of Tea actually **increases** the probability of Coffee!  
> For this rule, confidence is misleading.

## Statistical independence

**Example:** Population of 1000 students
- 600 know how to swim ($S$)
- 700 know how to bike ($B$)
- 420 know both ($S \land B$)

$$
P(S \land B) = \frac{420}{1000} = 0.42
$$

$$
P(S) \times P(B) = 0.6 \times 0.7 = 0.42
$$

> **Statistical independence:** $P(S \land B) = P(S) \times P(B)$
>
> - $P(S \land B) > P(S) \times P(B)$ → **Positively correlated**
> - $P(S \land B) < P(S) \times P(B)$ → **Negatively correlated**

## Lift
$$
\text{lift}(A \rightarrow C) = \frac{\text{conf}(A \rightarrow C)}{\text{supp}(C)} = \frac{P(A, C)}{P(A) \times P(C)}
$$

- Evaluates to **1** for independence
- **Insensitive to rule direction**
- Ratio of true cases w.r.t. independence

##  Leverage

$$
\text{leverage}(A \rightarrow C) = P(A, C) - P(A) \times P(C) = \text{supp}(A \cup C) - \text{supp}(A) \times \text{supp}(C)
$$

- Evaluates to **0** for independence
- **Insensitive to rule direction**
- Number of additional cases w.r.t. independence

##  Conviction

$$
\text{conv}(A \rightarrow C) = \frac{1 - \text{supp}(C)}{1 - \text{conf}(A \rightarrow C)} = \frac{P(A)(1 - P(C))}{P(A) - P(A, C)}
$$

- Infinite if the rule is always true
- **Sensitive to rule direction**
- Ratio of expected frequency that $A$ occurs without $C$ (if independent) divided by observed frequency of incorrect predictions
- Also called **novelty**

## Intuition about measures

- **Higher support** → rule applies to more records
- **Higher confidence** → chance that the rule is true for some record is higher
- **Higher lift** → chance that the rule is just a coincidence is lower
- **Higher conviction** → the rule is violated less often than it would be if antecedent and consequent were independent

![](theory/images/Screenshot%202025-12-26%20alle%2017.26.33.png)

## Lift: Relative association strength

### Practical meaning
How many times the presence of the consequent is **inflated** if the antecedent is present.

> Measures departure from statistical independence

### Key limitations
- **Inflated for rare items** (can give misleadingly high values for infrequent items)
- **No notion of absolute business impact** (only relative measure)

## Leverage: Absolute incremental effect

$$
\text{Leverage}(A \rightarrow B) = P(A \cap B) - P(A)P(B)
$$

### Practical meaning
If leverage = 0.04: "4% of all transactions contain the pair because of the association"

### Business interpretation
In 1M transactions, this rule explains **40,000 extra joint purchases** beyond what would be expected by chance.

> Measures **incremental volume**, not correlation

## Conviction: Rule reliability

$$
\text{Conviction}(A \rightarrow B) = \frac{P(A)P(\neg B)}{P(A \cap \neg B)}
$$

### Practical meaning
How strongly the rule **avoids being wrong** (penalizes cases where $A$ occurs but $B$ does not)

## Visual intuition: Confidence vs Conviction

| | **Confidence** | **Conviction** |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| **What it measures** | $P(B \mid A)$ | $\frac{\text{expected failures under independence}}{\text{observed failures}}$ |
| **Perspective** | Looks only at successes | Compares failures to independence baseline |
| **Intuition** | "When I recommend, how often am I right?" | "How surprising is it when the recommendation fails?" |
| **Warning sign** | - | High confidence + low conviction → frequent false positives |

## When lift is misleading

### Counterexample 1: Rare items
Assume:
- $P(A) = 0.01$, $P(B) = 0.01$, $P(A \cap B) = 0.005$

$$
\text{Lift} = \frac{0.005}{0.0001} = 50
$$

> **Problem:** Extremely high lift but only 0.5% of transactions affected
>
> **Business reality:** Rule is statistically strong but commercially irrelevant

### Counterexample 2: Popular items
Assume:
- $P(A) = 0.80$, $P(B) = 0.70$, $P(A \cap B) = 0.60$

$$
\text{Lift} \approx 1.07
$$

> **Observation:** Lift suggests weak association
>
> **Insight:** Yet 60% of users satisfy the rule!
>
> Lift **undervalues** rules involving popular items

## Mapping metrics to recommender KPIs

| Metric | Measures | Related KPI |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| **Lift** | Relative predictiveness | CTR uplift |
| **Leverage** | Incremental volume | Revenue lift |
| **Conviction** | Error avoidance | Precision@K |
| **Confidence** | Success frequency | Hit Rate |
| **Support** | Coverage | Catalog utilization |

## Practical guidance

### Use lift for discovery and filtering
- Identifies statistically interesting associations
- Good for finding novel patterns

### Use leverage for revenue prioritization
- Quantifies absolute business impact
- Directly translates to potential revenue

### Use conviction for recommendation reliability
- Measures how consistently the rule holds
- Important for user trust and experience

> **Rule of thumb:**  
> High lift ∧ high leverage ∧ high conviction → production-ready rule

## Conclusion on measures

- Many measures exist beyond the four presented
- **Confidence** is usually the base tool
- Other measures can test and filter confidence results
- **No single perfect measure** – use combination based on business goals

## Multidimensional association rules

### Comparison: Mono- vs multi-dimensional

| | **Mono-dimensional** | **Multi-dimensional** |
|------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **Event** | Transaction | Tuple |
| **Event description** | Items A, B, C are together in a transaction | Attribute A has value a, B has value b, C has value c in a tuple |

### Example: Sensor data
Look for rules like: `CO = high AND Tin Oxide = high → Titanium = high`

**Use case:** If one sensor is unavailable, guess its qualitative value from others.

### Handling quantitative attributes
Too many distinct values for direct multi/mono transformation → need **discretization**.

**Approaches:**
- Equifrequency binning
- Mono-dimensional clustering for optimal covering

> **Key insight:**  
> Association rules can involve items at different qualitative levels after discretization

## Multilevel association rules

### The challenge
A real market basket database can include **tens of thousands** of distinct items.

Need to find trade-off between:
- **General reasoning** (higher-level patterns)
- **Detailed reasoning** (specific item combinations)

> **Key:** Choose the right level of abstraction

### Solution: Concept hierarchy
Common background knowledge is the organization of items into a **hierarchy of concepts**:
- Can be easily coded in transactions
- Helps choose the right level of abstraction

## Support in multilevel association rules

### From specialized to general
Example: `(apple → milk) → (fruit → dairy)`

> **Support increases** (in general)
>
> New rules can become interesting at higher levels

### From general to specialized
Example: `(fruit → dairy) → (apple → milk)`

> **Support decreases** (in general)
>
> Support can go under the threshold at lower levels

## Confidence in multilevel association rules

A level change can influence confidence **in any direction** (increase, decrease, or remain similar).

> **Redundancy rule:**  
> If a specialized rule has (approximately) the same confidence as the general one, then it is **redundant**

### Example
- 1000 transactions
- 80 with milk and bread
- 114 with milk
- 20 with low-fat milk and bread
- 28 with low-fat milk

**Rule a:** `milk → bread`  
- Support = 8%  
- Confidence = 70%

**Rule b:** `low-fat milk → bread`  
- Support = 2%  
- Confidence = 71%

> Since rule b) has almost the same confidence as rule a) and is a descendant,  
> **rule b) is redundant**

## Mining multilevel association rules

### Approach: Top-down exploration
1. Look for frequent itemsets at **each level of abstraction**, top-down
2. Each level requires a new run of the rule discovery algorithm
3. **Decrease the support threshold** in lower levels (to account for decreasing support)

This hierarchical approach helps manage the complexity while maintaining meaningful patterns at appropriate abstraction levels.
